## 2. The fable (full text)

### The Beaver Who Kept Losing the River

There was once a young beaver named Pip who lived on a bend of a bright, quick river.

Pip wanted to build a dam. Not a small one. A good one, with a still pool behind it where the water would slow down and the light would settle and she could finally hear herself think.

So one morning she began. She cut a birch sapling and dragged it to the shallows. She wedged it against a stone. She went back for a second one.

On the way, a fish flashed silver in the current, and Pip followed it for a while just to see where it went. When she came back to the bank, the sun had moved. She looked at the single birch log in the shallows and could not remember which end she had meant to build from.

"Well," said Pip, "I'll start fresh." And she began a new dam a little way upstream.

The next day a storm came, and she spent it patching her den. The day after, a duckling got tangled in reeds and Pip stayed with it until its mother came. The day after that, she woke up tired in a way she could not explain, and she lay in the shallows and watched the clouds.

Each time she returned to the river, she found a beginning she did not recognize. A log here. Two logs there. A neat little pile of stones with no dam around it. Each time, she felt a small hot shame in her chest, and each time she said, "Well. I'll start fresh," and began again somewhere new.

By midsummer the bank was littered with beginnings. Seven half-dams. Nine. Pip stopped counting.

An old heron who fished that stretch of river had been watching all season. Herons are patient, and they notice things. One evening, as Pip sat with her chin on her paws staring at her ninth beginning, the heron folded down beside her.

"You work harder than any creature on this river," the heron said.

"I have nothing to show for it," said Pip.

"You have nine dams."

"I have nine *starts*. That is not the same."

The heron was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "May I tell you what I see? I do not see a beaver who cannot finish. I see a beaver who has built the same dam nine times, because every time she leaves, she takes the whole river with her in her head, and when she comes back, it has all run out."

Pip said nothing. It was true, and it had never once occurred to her.

"Fish flash," the heron went on. "Storms come. Ducklings get tangled. Tired days arrive without asking. You are not going to stop leaving, little one. Nobody on this river stops leaving. But you could stop starting over."

"How?"

"Before you go, whatever pulls you, put one bright stone on the log you will pick up next. Not a promise. Not a plan. Just a place. So that when you come back, the river does not have to explain itself to you all over again."

Pip thought this sounded too small to matter. But she was tired of starting fresh, so the next morning she tried it.

She laid a log. She reached for another. A dragonfly went past, and she felt the old tug, and before she followed it she set a white stone on the end of the log she had just placed. Then she went.

She was gone a long while. When she came back, the sun had moved and her mind was as empty of the dam as ever. But there was the white stone, sitting on the log, and it said, without a word: *here. You were here. Start here.*

So she did.

She still left. She left for fish and for storms and for tired days and for one whole afternoon of watching an ant carry a seed. The stone did not stop any of that. It only waited.

And because she came back to the same dam instead of a new one, the dam grew. Log by log, in no particular rhythm, across the whole rest of the summer.

By the first cold morning of autumn there was a still pool behind it, just as she had wanted, with the light settled on the water and the sound of the river gone quiet enough to think.

The heron came by and stood in the shallows of it.

"You finished," she said.

"I didn't stop leaving," said Pip.

"No," said the heron. "You stopped losing the river."

---

*You do not have to stop leaving. You only have to leave yourself a place to come back to.*

## 3. Card-length version (Spark Note format)

The daily Spark card allows roughly 180 words in the story section, so the full fable cannot fit on the collapsed card. The version below fits the card. The full fable belongs in the "Tell Me More" second layer, which the record type already supports (see section 8).

**Title:** The Beaver Who Kept Losing the River
**Short title:** The Beaver and the River
**Teaser:** A young beaver built the same dam nine times before an old heron showed her the one thing she was missing.

**Story (card):**
Pip the beaver wanted to build a dam with a still pool behind it, quiet enough to think. She started well. Then a fish flashed, or a storm came, or a duckling needed help, or a tired day arrived without asking. Each time she came back, she could not remember where she had been, felt a small hot shame, and started fresh somewhere new. By midsummer the bank held nine half-dams. An old heron who had watched all season said, "You have not failed to finish. You have built the same dam nine times, because each time you leave, you take the whole river with you in your head." Her advice was small: before you go, set one bright stone on the log you will pick up next. Not a promise. Just a place. Pip still left, for fish and storms and tired days. But now she came back to the same dam. By autumn, the pool was still.

**Impact:**
Interruption is not the enemy of finishing. Starting over is. Most of what feels like "I can't finish things" is really "I lose my place, and restarting feels easier than finding it." A visible restart point, left before you go, costs almost nothing and turns nine beginnings into one dam. This is why Spark Estate cares so much about return: not to keep you from leaving, but so the river never has to explain itself to you twice.

**Spark It (application):**
What is one thing you keep starting over instead of returning to? Before you step away from it next, leave one bright stone: a single line that says where you were and what comes next. Then let yourself leave. Afterward, save a short note about what you noticed when you came back.

## 4. Why this fable, and why this shape

### The problem the fable is for

The ADHD Founder Experience Standards name the deeper problems Spark Estate exists to address, including working-memory loss, interruption and task-switching costs, "returning without remembering the prior mental state," and "shame caused by systems that treat inconsistency as failure." This fable is aimed precisely at that cluster. It does not address time blindness, decision overload, or capture, which are candidates for other fables in a series.

### Decisions embedded in the story

- **The beaver is not cured.** Pip leaves just as often at the end as at the beginning. The fable refuses the "learn discipline" arc. This keeps faith with the rule against forced completion, streaks, and productivity moralizing.
- **The heron does not lecture.** She observes, reframes ("you have built the same dam nine times"), and offers one tiny, concrete action. This mirrors the intended companion tone: "a trusted person sitting beside the founder, not a manager, evaluator, lecturer, or cheerleader."
- **Shame is named, then dissolved by reframing rather than reassurance.** Pip's "small hot shame" is real and is not argued away. It disappears when the situation is understood differently. This protects dignity without forced positivity.
- **The advice is one action, not a system.** "Put one bright stone on the log you will pick up next" is the fable's entire method. This honors "one clear next action" and the cognitive load budget.
- **The moral is about return, not effort.** The last line is a permission, not an instruction. It aligns with "Return without reconstruction" and "Calm completion."
- **Every departure is legitimate.** Fish, storm, duckling, tired day, an ant with a seed. Some are curiosity, some are care, some are capacity. None is treated as a failure. The tired day in particular is there for low-energy and pain days.
- **No numbers to track, no streak.** The dam grows "in no particular rhythm." This was deliberate.

### The "So what?" test

- **Why does this matter?** It gives a member a picture of their own pattern that is kind and accurate at the same time.
- **What becomes easier?** Leaving a restart point stops feeling like extra admin and starts feeling like setting down a stone.
- **What can the member do or feel because this exists?** Recognize that the problem was never "finishing," and try one small return ritual without being told to be more consistent.

### Success test (from the ADHD Founder Experience Standards)

A member finishing the card should be able to say: "I know what this is. I know why it matters. I know what to do next. I can come back without starting over. This is helping me, not judging me." The story, impact, and Spark It sections were written to hit each of those in turn.

## 5. Experience flow (if published as a Spark)

This describes how a member would encounter the fable using surfaces that already exist in Spark Estate. No new room, route, or destination is proposed.

**Flow A: Today's Spark (primary, existing surface)**

1. The member opens the Estate on an ordinary day. Today's Spark card appears in its usual place with the short title "The Beaver and the River" and the teaser.
2. The member opens the card. They read the card-length story (about a minute). The Impact section follows, then Spark It.
3. Optionally, the member taps "Tell Me More." The full fable appears in the second layer, along with a short "try this" line. Reading the whole fable takes three to four minutes.
4. The member may save the card to My Collection, or simply close it. Nothing is required. No rating, no note, no completion step.
5. If the member does try the "bright stone" idea, the Spark It section suggests saving a short note. This uses the existing save-a-note path and creates no task, project, or reminder.

**What the member sees and feels:** a story, not a tip. A reframe they can recognize themselves in, without being named as the problem. One small thing to try, offered rather than assigned.

**Flow B: Return after absence (Version 2 candidate, not proposed for now)**

The fable's subject matches the "return without reconstruction" moment exactly. A future version could let the companion offer this fable once, gently, when a member returns after a long absence and appears to be about to restart work that already has a return point. This is noted for later only. It requires consent design and a rule for never using a story as a nudge or a reproach. Do not build now.

## 6. Decisions made and decisions reserved

### Decisions made on your behalf (routine, reversible)

| Decision | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Subject of the fable | Losing your place and restarting, versus returning | Closest match to the Estate's "return without reconstruction" promise, and to the stated problem list |
| Protagonist | A beaver, Pip | Building a dam is visibly incremental and visibly interruptible. Chosen over the more familiar "magpie who collects shiny things," which risks reading as a cliché about distraction |

| Record status | `draft` | Per the library workflow, nothing enters daily selection until you have read it and moved it to review, then active |
